Economy isn't money. It's the collective set of strategies people use to meet their needs: food, water, shelter, community, meaning, care. That includes the formal economy (jobs, currency, markets, GDP) and the informal economy (childcare, eldercare, growing food, gift-giving, mutual aid). The informal economy is usually invisible to formal measurement, but GDP and official economic measures miss a lot. This review starts with a refusal of that exclusion: define economy as need-meeting, and the gift economy and the solidarity economy stop looking like alternatives to "real" economics. They're the original economics.

The critique of the dominant economy runs like this: 

- Profit requires engineered scarcity.

- Growth is mandated through interest-bearing debt.

- A person's worth is tied to their productivity.

- Racial division is used to prevent collective power.

- Land, labor, and human beings are extracted without being replenished.


This means **an economy built only around money will always undercount the work that actually keeps people alive.**


As we think about how to welcome people to Taabir, a planned resource center in Irving, TX, its shape needs to physically reflect the value of human life. Part of this has to be passive, to not transact, to just meet the need. Taabir is a place to exist, to belong, to be human without consumption attached. Every other function, vocational training, co-working, the café, radiates outward from that core.

The harder, ongoing question: how does Taabir stay an informal economy institution (gift, belonging, mutual aid) while operating inside formal economy structures (property ownership, payroll, grants)? That tension does not resolve once. It has to be managed continuously.
