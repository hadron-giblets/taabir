# Field Notes

Going to four settings, one open question each. None of this would surface in a conventional needs assessment.

**Belt Line Kroger, 75061.** Nine shoppers, mostly Hispanic and white women in their 30s to 60s, talked to in the store and the checkout line. I asked, *do you like this Kroger?* The answer was no, and it wasn't about the groceries. Solicitors, both corporate and unhoused, came up in nearly every conversation. Several shoppers described specific coping tactics: weaving through aisles to avoid people, or heading straight to the back of the store. More than one described the trip itself as exhausting. One man, new to the neighborhood, called it "claustrophobic." Bank representatives trail shoppers through the aisles. There's a rumor that ICE has shown up. Those who can manage it drive to the Kroger in Las Colinas or to Joe V's Smart Shop instead. The takeaway: it is stressful to get needs met. 

**South Irving Library.** Two Irving ISD staff members. I asked, *what are the biggest challenges is for your students?* Families have been doubling and tripling up just to stay housed, not homeless by any official count, but close enough that school staff have effectively become informal tenant-rights advocates. Attendance drops when it rains. Transit gaps quietly close off internship opportunities before students ever get the chance. The takeaway: education, transit, and housing aren't separate problems here, they are one problem wearing three names.

**West Irving Library.** Two Information Desk librarians. I asked, *what do people love about the library?* People come for the space because it is free, and they do not need to do anything to belong there. People like a place where they can do nothing, hang out late (the library is open on weekdays from 10 am to 9 pm), and charge their devices. Their programming does not require a library card and they see strong usage from children, teens, and adults. The library makes no money of its own, and is entirely dependent on city funding. As a primary resource, the library is a designated cooling center and they distribute free water when the city supplies it. Today's feels like temperature peaked around 113 degrees, but the free water distribution isn't deemed necessary by the city yet. This is not good. But the library cannot even buy its own water to give out. The takeaway: residents have an appetite for a welcoming room, and Taabir has an opportunity in being able to do more for people if it is thoughtful about funding.

**It'll Do Club, Church goth night.** Eight respondents at the bar and smoking patio, in their 20s to 40s, white, Black, and Hispanic. There are no real bars in 75061, just a few restaurant-bar combinations, so this conversation happened at a goth night across town. I asked, *what's your favorite place in DFW? Where do you go most often?* The answers clustered hard around one pattern: low-cost or membership-based, low-pressure, no ask, come back whenever. People named the Wylie Rec Center's climbing wall, downtown Dallas museums on a membership, an Ulta Beauty membership, wherever someone happened to bring a chessboard, thrift store discount days, and the library. The takeaway: people want somewhere to play.

Together, these conversations point to how Taabir's community space needs to work: not a service someone gets referred to, but a place someone chooses to keep coming back to.
