# How an Invitation Ends

Every governance document in this genre covers hiring. The same documents cover harm: transformative accountability, repair, removal for cause. Let's talk about the ending that is nobody's fault. No harm, no violation, no villain but a word that comes up all the time in all sort of orgs — a bad "fit."

## How the role was made

The Community Connector role was drafted at velocity, mid-urgency while we planned a community gathering, and as a long list of needs: attend in-person community meetings, run the needs assessment while it was still imagined as a survey, handle outreach and correspondence. There was a consideration of paying the role by output, which felt uncomfortable and hierarchical, so it carried the same monthly stipend as the rest of the staff got. Underneath all the drafting sat the real want, the one that caused the trouble later: we did not want an employee to manage. We wanted another hand on the team.

"Hand on the team," what does that mean? It helps to say plainly what the team actually is. Taabir's Cohort I does not have coherent roles. We have things we ended up taking ownership of based on strengths and shapes we recognized while also constantly doing work we had never done before or never done this way: investor decks, workshops, site plans, operating expense projections, whatever the week demanded. So **even the work of figuring out your work is work.** 

Someone willing and ready to be directed isn't contributing to that labor, isn't even visible in it. And in a four person call, quiet can read as divestment even when it comes from listening.

The needs assessment stalled here. It sat sluggish inside the plan-as-imagined: make a form, advertise the form, email people about the form. The fix was to abandon the form-shaped plan and just go out and bother people where they are. That fieldwork produced the real commitment to area that makes all this organizing work meaningful. It also made the Connector role's central deliverables obsolete, and the gap between the role as drafted and the work as actually done became the story.

## The conversation

This was set up like any other meeting. The first half asked (offered) every avenue: is something burdensome on your plate, is there a hardship, is there a way we can help, is anything unclear. Those offers were real. The answer was no to all of them, plus one more thing: an offer to do more, if that was needed.

That answer forced the pivot. There were three things: 
- the role's own expectations, namely attendance at multiple community meetings, could not honestly be promised to satisfaction from that point forward;
- the stipend, while genuinely helping its recipient, was money that could go to someone less privileged, someone who needed it more and would do more for it; and
- being paid for community organizing work is a privilege, and shouldn't be taken for granted. 

And ultimately both sides made the same admission that by this definition, the role's needs could not be fulfilled.

The organizational term for this conversation is "fit," but that's softening the issue. It was a rhetorical path — a sequence of framings that led, step by step, to the one conclusion that could end the stipend. It wasn't a manipulation. Everyone in the room meant well, and every sentence said was honest. But it was not a mechanism either. A mechanism has criteria defined before the conversation. We discovered ours inside it and named the result after the fact. The ending was just; the process was improvised; both are true.

## The audit, institutionalized

The specific failures:

**The hire was friendship-shaped.** The match came out of an old and good relationship. We didn't confirm the role's sprawl against the person's demonstrated practice, partly because—

**We had no bandwidth to be evaluative.** The role was so sprawling that diligence wasn't confirmable by any process we had. Interviews, references, trial periods felt like bureaucracy, but it would've been better for everyone in some measure.

**Contracts without tax status enforce nothing.** We have fellow agreements and contracts on paper. Under fiscal sponsorship, the only lever that actually exists is the stipend itself. Cutting it was the entire enforcement mechanism but pay is more than reward. It touches a lot of things, and for an org that values compensation, the reality is that we are thin in a lot of ways.

**The Connector should have been from South Irving.** The bylaws promise community representatives in the governance structure. We don't have one. The single role purpose-built for community connection was filled through our network, not from the neighborhood, so the lack of connectivity showed. We networked the role when we should have rooted it. Until someone from South Irving holds real power in this organization, the covenant's community accountability language is empty.

**Salary-by-need contains a trap.** LIFT Economy talks about how they set salaries based on need, and it sounds beautiful until the organization gets to decide whose need counts. I think the argument "this money should go to someone who needs it more" becomes a means-test. An organization that refuses means-testing at its front door can't run its payroll as a competition of need. See [[Compensation as Values Made Visible]] for the structural version of this issue.

## What we owe

- Define the role's real outputs before the hire, not during the exit. 
- Hire from the community.
- Treat evaluation as a kindness, clarity as care. We have to find the way to take time with people.
- Structure how we do this before we're in the meeting. Have transparency along the way.

And give roles what [[Conditions, Not Content]] gives projects: an exit. An invitation without an exit isn't an invitation. It's a baited trap.
