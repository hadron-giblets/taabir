[[Economic Zones of Transaction|Gift and barter]] create dense relational networks, and cash breaks them down. LIFT gives the example of when money was introduced into Inuit whale harvest practices, a working system of mutual aid collapsed into something much thinner in a short time.

DFW is enormous. South Irving is small. The gathering room has to serve its immediate neighborhood and be genuinely of that place, but it cannot function as an island either. South Irving residents need connection to resources beyond South Irving to get their needs met reliably.

Here, social connectivity is economic connectivity. When Taabir connects a resident to a cooperative grocery in Oak Cliff, a mutual aid network in South Dallas, or a vocational program in Fort Worth, that is still need-meeting without cash changing hands, even though it happens outside the building. The organization works as a referral system.

The bylaws' ecological commitment is written loosely right now, on purpose: prefer vendors and materials aligned with ecological sustainability "even when such preference requires additional effort or nominal additional cost" ([[Bylaws#Ecological Commitment]]). That looseness is a placeholder, not a final position. A sharper future version of that clause, something like committing to source a set percentage of operational needs from within a given radius and prioritizing cooperatives, would help create real demand for the cooperative ecosystem Taabir is trying to help build.

The gathering room is small, and the needs it can meet directly are limited. But the connections it can facilitate (relationships, introductions, knowledge passed from one person to another) do not face that same limitation.
