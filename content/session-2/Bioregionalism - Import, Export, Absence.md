title: "Bioregionalism: Import, Export, Absence"

DFW is not really a bioregion. It is a metro area that sits at the edge of the Blackland Prairie ecoregion and the Eastern Cross Timbers, cut through by the Trinity River watershed, which is what more ecologically binds our region together. But we will just call it DFW, a big and recognizable shape.

DFW is a huge, wealthy, well-connected metro, and most of the goods or services we want and need exist somewhere in the region. But the resources we produce here are usually not used close to where they are needed.

Energy I think most of all! Natural gas generates the largest share of ERCOT's power, and DFW's own footprint includes Comanche Peak, a nuclear plant just southwest of Fort Worth, so some baseload generation is close by. But wind is a West Texas and Panhandle resource, imported over transmission lines, and solar is similar: utility-scale solar farms cluster where land is cheap and consistently sunny, mostly west and south of DFW. The sun that falls on DFW rooftops every day is a locally abundant resource that has barely been built out because of the market structure.

Austin and San Antonio have publicly owned municipal utilities that pushed rooftop solar hard for over a decade, while DFW runs on deregulated Oncor territory, where incentives were thin until recently. Most homeowners I know would love solar if they believed they could reliably pay for it. Dallas only launched a group rooftop solar purchasing program this year.

Food follows a similar pattern, which [[The Cup of Tea, Turned Upside Down - Being Taught]] already approaches from a different angle. Dallas County alone has around 840 farms on nearly 84,000 acres, with high-quality soil suited to fruit and vegetable production, but most of that acreage goes to livestock, row crops, and hay instead of produce. So the fresh produce on a DFW plate travels in from California, Mexico, or the Midwest, not because the land cannot grow it, but because the regional agricultural economy is built around commodity crops and cattle instead.

And still, based on Feeding America's study, DFW ranks third among U.S. metros for the share of people facing hunger, even as restaurants and institutions here throw out millions of pounds of edible food a year. The capacity and the need are both local. The system connecting them is not.

Water is a surprising opportunity for regenerative capture that is leaving the bioregion instead of cycling inside it. Fort Worth and Dallas treat their wastewater to full state and federal standards and release it into the Trinity River, where it flows downstream and becomes a drinking water source for Houston via Lake Livingston. Locally, reuse infrastructure is thin by comparison. Fort Worth runs a smallish non-potable reclaimed water program, and the North Texas Municipal Water District built an engineered wetland on the Trinity's East Fork back in 2009 for indirect local reuse, but there is nothing here like San Antonio's large purple pipe network, which irrigates golf courses, keeps the River Walk flowing, and supplies industrial users. DFW finishes using its water and the reclaimed value goes a couple hundred miles away.

Manufactured goods are the least surprising gap. Endemic to this whole country, clothing, furniture, consumer electronics, most durable goods, arrive through global supply chains, the [[Economic Zones of Transaction]] Zone 4 end of the spectrum. DFW does still make things, aerospace and defense, some automotive assembly, steel, but not the goods a household is using.

What ties these together is not scarcity. It is that DFW routinely has the underlying resource, sun, fertile soil, treated water, and lacks the local infrastructure or institutional push to keep the value of that resource circulating close to home instead of exporting it, whether that export is literal, like wastewater flowing to Houston, or structural, like produce dollars flowing to California growers.

Taabir is not positioned to close any of these gaps at the scale of the metro, but it can make closer points of connection to programs for solar; support an urban ag group (beautiful examples in the metroplex include Oak Cliff Veggie Project and Urban Dallas Farm); recapture rainwater. Taabir can model smaller-scale regenerative practices at the building's own footprint.

And the bylaws' ecological commitment clause is exactly where a future version could get specific: a stated preference for vendors and materials sourced within the region, prioritizing cooperatives, would turn Taabir's own purchasing into a small, real source of demand for the local regenerative economy this note keeps finding thin.

**Open questions for Taabir:**
- Where else is DFW finishing the production of something valuable and then exporting it, the way it does with treated wastewater?
- Which of these gaps could a building like Taabir make an impact on? What can we teach people to do with this information?
- What would be the conditions added to strengthen the ecological commitment clause in the bylaws? Are we sourcing in this community, bioregion, this state?
