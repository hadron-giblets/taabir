# Built From Absence

Each of the four instructors' stories contains a rupture, a moment when the path they were on revealed itself as wrong. The rupture is a persistent kind of painful, and part of what makes it painful is that the systems producing it made it close to inevitable. What follows the rupture, in every account, is a long search for something that actually fits.

Why do any of this? Why build a cooperative? Why rewrite bylaws? Why sit with conflict instead of walking away? Why choose less when more was the only safety you ever knew?

The answer the MBA offers is not an argument. But I think it is the feeling that the world can be different than what was done to us.

That refusal  to be hurtful can coexist with bleakness, and doubt, and passive suicidality. In fact, those who carry that feeling (who know viscerally that the world as it exists is not safe, not welcoming, not designed for their flourishing) are often the ones who build the alternatives most carefully. Because they know what is missing. They know what it feels like to be in a room and not feel welcome in your own skin. So they build rooms that are different.

Taabir isn't built from abundance. It's built from knowing what absence feels like. The welcoming room exists because someone knows what it means to need welcome and not receive it. A free, no-conditions gathering space exists because someone knows that cost, financial or emotional, the cost of having to prove you deserve to be there, is a barrier that excludes exactly the people who need a place to sit.

The bylaws naming burnout as a structural failure rather than a personal one comes from someone knowing what burnout feels like from the inside. Consent-based governance comes from someone knowing what it feels like to have decisions made over you instead of with you. The abundance practice comes from someone knowing that scarcity isn't only economic. It's a posture toward the world, a bracing against loss, and it can become so habitual it starts to feel like a personality.

People who have experienced what is missing often carry an unusually precise vision of what could replace it. That precision is part of why Taabir needs its own community involved in shaping it, not just serving it.

The systems change themes in this lesson, circularity, redefining progress, localization, recommoning, definancialization, can feel abstract. But they are descriptions of a world in which a child who feels unloved might find a room that says: you belong here, no conditions. 

That is circularity: nothing wasted, including people. 

That is redefining progress: not income, but belonging. 

That is recommoning: space that belongs to everyone because it is owned by no one.

That is definancialization: a place where your presence does not require a transaction.

The macro and the micro are the same thing.
