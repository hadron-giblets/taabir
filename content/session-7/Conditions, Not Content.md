# Conditions, Not Content

This sentence from [[Session 7 - Visioning]] is a tough one: **the job is creating the conditions for other people's visions to emerge.**

Other people to need to bring their visioning to Taabir to create something real, new, better than business-as-usual economics in DFW. The limited staff of Taabir is limited in visions it can sustain itself. So the resource center's main resource is not the programs themselves but the conditions to make them your own.

## What a real thing gets

If someone brings Taabir a real project, Taabir develops obligations:

**A name and a budget line.** Projects that live only in enthusiasm also die there. If there's no money, the obligation is a quick no. A slow maybe would be theft.

**Hours logged as equity.** This one comes from the bylaws. Unpaid and underpaid hours are recorded as community investment, and if the organization ever dissolves with assets, recorded hours inform the distribution ([[Bylaws#Time as Equity Contribution]]). A fellow's project hours are stake in Taabir, and it's the organization's obligation to keep track.

**The first question.** Does anything here stay *free in a meaningful way* (preventing spend of cost, time, information, other forms of economics) for the community?

**An exit.** Under the fiscal sponsorship agreement, intellectual property created by project workers stays with the project ([[Fiscal Sponsorship Agreement]]). If the café is dreamed as a co-op and it leaves, the models are open-sourced so anyone can take them. A project outgrowing Taabir is success.

## Role models

**Highlander.** The Highlander Folk School, in New Market, TN ran a campus with beds, food, meeting halls, workshops. People brought their own fights — it hosted the Appalachian coal miners first, then the civil rights movement. Rosa Parks sat in a Highlander workshop in the summer of 1955, just months before Montgomery. Septima Clark built the citizenship schools there that taught literacy and civics for Black voters across the South. It began as a Highlander extension program, and in 1961 they were handed to the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and scaled up to about 900 schools and hundreds of thousands of new registered voters. Highlander didn't own it. It gave the resources, and it let the content leave and change the country.

The state of Tennessee knew exactly how effective it was: it revoked Highlander's charter and seized its property in 1960. The school reopened the next day under a new name and lives to this day.

**Ujima.** In the Boston Ujima Project, it's members, not the staff, who vote on which businesses and projects get financed. The organization builds the mechanism; the community supplies the vision. Like Highlander it supplies the resources, in this case with capital instead of classrooms.

## What Taabir wants

These are some of the projects we've envisioned for someone to actualize and suffuse with their own vision:

**The asset map.** Let's understand deeply the history of South Irving, what informal structures have grown and lived there. We've mapped wants in the casual, relational way but what about mapping the community strengths? Divested areas always suffer from an imbalanced ratio of bad to good news, but there are a lot of local solutions that don't get found. This would be teaching the org. Once the existing care networks are visible, the kitchen, the shelf, the lending circle, the theatre all become designable instead of guessed.

**A Documenters program.** The Dallas Free Press pays residents to cover public meetings in Dallas through its Documenters program. A Taabir version is income for community members, an accountability layer over the land bank and the zoning board, and gives Taabir a significant media function in one project. It's also a deeply time-consuming one. Thinking about what makes a project "enough" even when it can never be complete.

**Lending circles with faith partners.** Can we support a money pool? What's the line between sustaining an informal practice and protecting it vs. over-documenting and interfering with it? This asks for partnership with religious institutions, working out where Taabir adds value to different communities.

## The uneven heart

Is everyone at Taabir equally invested in the welcoming room? No. I write that it's the heart of everything here because it is for me and the people I know and who I most often talk to. Because I, like many, would struggle to ask for help anywhere. 

During the community needs assessment, I spent an hour working up the courage to talk to two librarians. An hour. Librarians. I could only do it because I could slink around the perimeter and feel oriented in the space and give up a hundred times before I succeeded once.

But when I did it, they told me plainly: people come because it is free, because you can do nothing, hang out late, charge your phone, belong without proving anything ([[The Welcome Room]]). The room is for the person who takes an hour. People who know what they want are already served by existing schools, incubators, resource centers because there is a full universe in the DFW metroplex. The room is a design for people who don't yet believe they're allowed to want ([[The Luxury of Preferences]]).

But a vision does not need everyone's passion, the most resources put into it, even the most advertising. **It needs protection.** It's the first step in our site sequencing plan, the precondition to everything because it welcomes in everything else, the individual dream of each individual person that will outgrow the room but first should see if they like sitting in it.
