# Why Organizations Avoid Conflict

Text-based communication strips out tone, body language, and relational context, and email in particular tends to escalate rather than de-escalate: the longer a message gets, the more surface area it gives to misinterpretation. People also carry more negative bias into text specifically with people they do not know well. The practical fix is simple: pick up the phone, send a voice message, get as close to a live conversation as the situation allows.

Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions offer a layered diagnostic read from the bottom up: absence of trust produces fear of conflict, which produces lack of commitment, which produces avoidance of accountability, which produces inattention to results. In shared-power organizations specifically, conflict avoidance is the default disease, since people tend to equate cooperation with never disagreeing. The pyramid names the actual cost of that equation: without trust, conflict feels unsafe; without healthy conflict, commitment stays vague; without commitment, accountability feels arbitrary to enforce; without accountability, the organization drifts without anyone quite deciding to let it.

The intervention starts with vulnerability, going first, naming the thing plainly: "I'm not holding up my commitments," or "I've been avoiding this conversation." That's leadership from the bottom up, making it safe for other people to be honest by being honest first.

Psychological safety does not mean niceness, and the research here is counterintuitive: safety produces more conflict, not less, but it is healthier conflict, and less hurtful when it happens. Unhealthy conflict is fighting to win, even through bullying or name-calling, and trust erodes even when the aggressor gets their way. Healthy conflict holds a dilemma together: I disagree, and I want to understand where you are coming from. The goal shifts from prevailing to understanding, and even when someone does not get what they wanted, feeling heard and cared for still increases trust rather than eroding it. Teams with real psychological safety outperform teams without it, because people share information, take real risks, and name problems while they are still small.
